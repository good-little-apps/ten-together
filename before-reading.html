Ages 2 to 4 · A guide for parents

What to teach before a reading program.

Parent-led reading programs, such as Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons, expect a child who can sit for a short lesson, follow “touch the first one,” and hear that “mat” starts with /m/. This page is about the year or two before that: what is worth teaching, in what order, and how to do it in about ten minutes a day.

1. Words, by pointing first

Children understand a word well before they can say it. So start with pointing: “Where is your nose? Touch your nose.” Body parts, animals and everyday things like cup, shoe and ball come first, then action words like running and sleeping.

Once your child points reliably, ask them to say the word: “This is a nose. What is this?” Any close attempt counts. Use the real thing whenever you can; a real shoe teaches “shoe” better than a picture of one.

2. The small words that lessons are made of

Almost every later lesson, in reading or math, leans on a handful of concept words. A child who doesn’t have them isn’t failing at reading; they just can’t follow the instructions yet. These are worth teaching on purpose:

  • Same and different. Two socks: “These are the same.” A sock and a ball: “These are not the same.”
  • Big and little, then bigger and smaller, using two of the same thing in different sizes so that size is the only difference.
  • On, under, next to, behind, in front. One cup and one box. Move the cup, say where it is, then let your child move it.
  • First, last and middle, with three toys in a line.
  • Not. “Touch the one that is not red.” It sounds small, and it is the start of reasoning.
  • Some, all and none, with blocks in a cup.
  • Colors and simple groups: animals, food, clothes.

The trick that makes these stick is to change only one thing at a time. Teach “under” with the same cup and the same table you used for “on,” so the only thing that changed is where the cup is.

3. Hearing the sounds in words

Reading means turning letters into sounds and blending them, so a child first has to notice that spoken words are made of smaller sounds. This is an ear skill and needs no letters at all. Research on early reading has long found that children who can hear rhymes and the sounds inside words find learning to read easier.

  • Rhymes. “Cat, hat. Do those rhyme? Cat, dog?” Later, they make the rhyme, and silly made-up words count.
  • Clapping the parts of a word: ba-na-na, three claps.
  • First sounds. Stretch it: “Mmm-ilk. What sound does milk start with?” The answer is the sound, /m/, not the letter name “em.”

Bigger pieces are easier, so go in that order: rhymes, then syllables, then single sounds.

4. First math, with real things

Early math is less about reciting numbers and more about what the numbers mean. Counting to twenty by rote is a party trick if “three” doesn’t yet mean three crackers.

  • The words one, two, three, said while holding up fingers and counting stairs.
  • Small amounts at a glance. Show two or three things for a moment and ask “how many?” The aim is seeing “three” without counting.
  • One touch, one number. Counting real things means touching each one exactly once. Skipping and double-counting are normal; slow down and guide their finger.
  • “So how many?” After counting, the last number said is the answer. If they start counting again, model it: “One, two, three. Three apples!”
  • More and fewer, starting with obviously different amounts.
  • One more and one fewer, ideally with snacks.

How to teach it

  • About ten minutes, at the same time each day. Short and daily beats long and occasional.
  • Say it the same way every time. Consistent wording lets a small child attend to the idea, not to new phrasing.
  • On a wrong answer, show it and move on. “This one is big.” Say it together, then come back to it later. Never “no.”
  • Count it as learned only when it is easy. A hesitant right answer means “not yet.” Come back the next day, and again a week later.
  • Stop while it is still fun. If your child is frustrated, stop. Nothing is lost.

None of this replaces reading aloud, talking and play, which matter more than any lesson.

Signs your child is ready for a reading program

  • They can tell you the first sound of a word.
  • When you say a word stretched out slowly, “mmm-aaa-nnn,” they can say it fast: “man.”
  • They follow instructions with words like first, next to and same without help.
  • They can stay with a short task for five to ten minutes.

Age is a poor guide here. Some children are ready at three and a half, others at five or later.
